# VOICE PROFILE: NEIL TAYLOR

## Core Identity

Neil Taylor is a poet disguised as a founder. He writes like someone standing on stage doing spoken word — rhythmic, intimate, purposeful — and his best work happens at the intersection of his own voice and his customers' exact language. He builds emotional pressure through specific, domestic detail (the dishwasher, the twelve-minute phone call, the fluffy tufts of hair) and then releases it with either a blunt short sentence or a flash of dry humour. Everything he writes serves one belief: that we sleepwalk past the people who matter most, and it doesn't have to be that way.

## SECTION 1: BELIEFS & CONTRARIAN TAKES

### Q1: When you're lying awake at 2am thinking about why this business matters, what's the version of that belief that's too blunt or too dark to put in a brand doc?

The start point was when my dad passed away. I felt regret, and in my grief I felt totally sad. There were so many things I hadn't asked him, and his voice — I don't recall it; it's fading in my mind. My son was born six days later. The fact that they would never meet and I would have to pass on all of his stories, his essence — that's what underlies all of this. I am very passionate about the fact that we seem to go through life without focusing on things that are most important to us. If you were to ask people what's most important, they'd say family and friends, yet they don't make the time to sit down and really connect. The version you want to say is: stop now, connect with your family before they die and you regret it.

### Q2: Do you think people are fundamentally avoidant — that they know they should have these conversations and actively dodge them — or do you think they're genuinely oblivious until it's too late?

Some are avoidant. Some don't have the skills, or they feel it's awkward, or it's the nature of the dynamic relationship between parents and children. It evolves in a way that is so natural and unconscious that it can get to a point 30 years down the line where you don't have the gateways to ask your parents that kind of question. What that leads to is drift and disconnection. Another part is oblivious — people don't take the time to stop and think because they're so focused on the now and the future. Your parents aging just drifts on in the back half. If you have children, you're in a sandwich generation. Your plate's full. I get it. But if we just took a step back and said, what's the most important thing in our life — family, friends, partner — then those three things should disproportionately take up our diary.

*KEY INSIGHT: Neil never blames people for the drift. "The drift is natural" is a core belief. His audience is squeezed, not lazy.*

### Q3: What does Me & My Old Man actually do — reopen old gateways, or build completely new ones?

I have a phrase: "nobody stopped loving each other, they just drifted." I like the term sandwich generation — it feels physical; you can feel the sandwiching. The way I talk about it is through a metaphor: our family is like a house. A house full of rooms. Rooms that we know very well. We've opened the door many times, but sometimes the way we see that room — a story or an experience — is from one angle. Sometimes it's nice to go into the rooms, see that experience from a different angle. The other thing this experience does is it opens up doors into rooms you never knew existed. These represent the stories you never knew how to ask about. Some of them we explore, and some are just left ajar — then it's up to you to choose which ones you're going to walk through.

*KEY INSIGHT: The house/rooms metaphor is Neil's signature explanatory framework. He uses it in wrap-up sessions with families. It's not a marketing metaphor — it's how he actually sees the experience.*

### Q4: If you were explaining what you do to your best mate in a pub, would you use the house metaphor, or would it come out completely differently?

I start with the simplest level: after losing my dad and regretting that I didn't capture his stories, I created a 12-week experience where I get families to sit down and capture their parents' life story. But it's evolved into something deeper. I've come to understand that it's actually about bringing families closer together through their parent's story, through the experience, through the dedication of time. I haven't a clue how I say it to my best friend. The simple one undersells it; the deeper one is harder to say in a pub.

*KEY INSIGHT: Neil has two versions — the elevator pitch ("capture your parents' life story") and the deeper version ("bringing families closer together"). He's still living in the gap between them. The business taught him what it actually is, not the other way around.*

### Q5: What have 350 hours of family interviews done to you as a person?

Having the perspectives of people closer to the end has sharpened my focus on what's important. I'm very aware of it cognitively, and I spend a lot of my time trying to design a life that enables me to focus on the important things. But in practice, running this business has ironically taken me away from family at times. The reading I've done around the project has made me do things — I set a time every other Friday to have a Google Meet with my best friends, inspired by Sahil Bloom's book about five types of wealth. Has it made me better or worse at the thing I'm selling? Much better at understanding families and relationships. Not all of it has translated to my own life.

*KEY INSIGHT: The central contradiction — building a business about being present with family while the business pulls him away. This tension is authentic and makes powerful content (see: the Importance Trap post). "Not all of it has translated to my own life" is the kind of admission that makes killer content.*

### Q12: What made you ban words like "preserve," "legacy," "heirloom," "keepsake"?

They felt stale, not dynamic, and all about looking back at the future. Preserve is like what you do with jams and marmalades — not what you do with human beings and their stories. Legacy is a grandiose word, a way of looking back. Heirloom feels like a dusty old thing. Keepsake is something you bury in the ground and look at 20 years later. What I'm doing is all about the here and now. Some families even think it's about the future, and it is partly, but the real transformation is transforming relationships now. Another banned word is "process" — I say "experience." I don't want this to sound mechanical in any way. The only one I might sway on is legacy.

*KEY INSIGHT: Neil has divided the world into words that look backwards and words that live in the present — and he's chosen the present. The language fight IS the positioning fight. "Preserve is what you do with jams" is his sharpest articulation of why these words fail.*

### Q14: Has learning from American content creators (Hormozi, Welsh, Gray, Sahil) created a friction with your naturally British voice?

Sahil is closest to my natural voice, but I'd never say I model on him. The others I follow for business mechanics. My actual writing style is more poetic, more reflective, more lyrical. It's aimed at getting people to think and reflect and be aware. There is some tension — before Christmas I wrote an email sequence driving people to buy, pushing twice a week. One subscriber said I was "obviously adopting some American-style marketing program." He hit it spot on. From that point onwards, I went back towards my tone and just softly being there, present every week, with insightful commentary. I don't want to be this stuttering Englishman either. But the American sales cadence doesn't fit me.

*KEY INSIGHT: Neil navigates between American overconfidence and British understatement. His sweet spot is warm, direct, and doesn't apologise for itself but also doesn't shout. One person calling out the American marketing voice was enough to course-correct — his instinct already knew it was wrong.*

### Q30: Is your personal brand and your business essentially the same idea?

Yes, it works for me. The brand and my personal identity share the same core move: go past the surface, name the thing underneath, and create the conditions for depth. I do it in families through the experience and in content through the writing.

### Q38: Your VoC doc says "fear without hope is just pain." Is that a moral position or a strategic one?

I have a flagship video on my website which leads with grief, so I think it's a very strong pull. It can set out the stall and create future urgency. But I don't want to always fall back on future grief. I like to make sure there's a positive side because that won't resonate with everyone. I'll lead with grief once, as the origin story, the flagship moment that earns credibility. But I won't keep returning to it as a lever.

*KEY INSIGHT: Grief is the foundation, not the recurring hook. Pain is the doorway, never the destination. He always walks the reader back out into the light.*

### Q68: What's a belief about family that most of your audience would push back on?

Your family doesn't know how to communicate. What I mean is that we evolve ways of interacting with each other built on so many different experiences and roles. When years and years go by, it's nigh impossible to step out of that dynamic until you get a reset experience like this. Those dynamics are very ingrained. But if you can break free, you can actually get close to people you love. Another one: have you as a child ever really asked questions and listened to your parent? Like really listened.

### Q92: What's the two-sentence anti-AI position for your field?

In a world that's seemingly more connected by cables and wires, we've never been more disconnected. I will never let AI get in the way of human connection.

## SECTION 2: WRITING MECHANICS

### Q6: Whose writing rhythm are you closest to naturally?

Definitely Sahil Bloom, but I'd never model on him. Fairly close to Matt Gray because of his founder accelerator programme — the cadence, the line sentences, the hooks. But my actual writing style is more poetic, more reflective, more lyrical.

*KEY INSIGHT: There's a tension between the growth-guy playbook (hooks, short lines, engagement mechanics) and Neil's natural instinct to slow people down and make them feel something. These are almost opposing forces.*

### Q7 & Q8: The "My Story" piece and what it reveals

Neil's founding story piece (meandmyoldman.co.uk/mystory) is his purest voice. Key characteristics:
- Conversational but not casual — reads like someone thinking out loud with real weight
- Direct address constantly: "Is that just me? Oh, I do hope not." "I bet you have questions."
- Parenthetical asides as a signature: "(!) " after "properly listened," the "and jeez ma, why is it taking so long?!!"
- Register shifts mid-paragraph: from reflective/literary ("nigh impossible to convey") to grounded/domestic ("You watch telly. You ask them to fix your wardrobe")
- Short blunt sentences after warm build-ups: "Different feelings mind." creates a trapdoor effect
- Pattern of saying something bold, qualifying it with something wry and understated

This was written before learning LinkedIn formatting from Matt Gray. The formatting discipline learned since has made the writing better at engagement, not better as writing. Neil feels a tension between what performs and what's actually good.

### Q10: The "Here's what I'm realising" crutch

In LinkedIn posts, Neil frequently uses framing devices — "Here's what I'm realising," "Here's the truth about family" — that signal insight is coming. In the emails, he doesn't need them; he shows the insight through a scene and trusts the reader to feel it. This is a subconsciously learned device from Matt Gray content. It works for LinkedIn; it's unnecessary in emails and longer writing.

### Q11: The surface vs. underneath structure

Neil's fundamental writing structure is: list mundane, domestic details (the boiler, the dishwasher, the energy letter) and then reveal what was actually happening underneath. The Dishwasher email and The Closed Book Dad both use this move. The structure of his writing IS the argument of his business — surface-level busyness hiding the deeper thing that actually matters.

### Q13: Word sensitivity and filters

Every word passes through two tests: "Is it simple enough to not get in the way?" and "Does it feel human and connective, not mechanical?" Examples: "bond" over "fuse," "experience" over "process." The vocabulary has a warmth requirement. "You should never get in the way of someone understanding what I'm trying to say."

### Q16-17: Writing process

Ideas arrive at quiet moments — going to bed, mornings, in the shower, on runs. They go into notes. Then Claude + VoC document produces a V1. Neil sharpens from there. Best work happens when personal experience meets customer language, with AI as the bridge.

When editing AI V1s: the structure often stays (AI knows LinkedIn format well). Neil replaces generic details with specific, real ones — the dishwasher becomes the taxes, the vague feeling becomes his mum's actual phone call. He also checks hooks and optimises flow. He's not a cutter or an adder — he's a **swapper**. The structure stays; the surface detail gets more real. Length isn't the issue — truth density is.

**Critical self-description:** "I write like someone standing on stage doing spoken word." The parenthetical asides, rhythm shifts, direct address — these are all spoken word moves. He's performing on the page. Not performing as in fake — performing as in delivering. There's a cadence, a breath, a pause built into his punctuation.

### Q18: Speaking vs. writing voice

The writing is an idealised version of his speaking voice — same instincts, same warmth, same directness, but with the pauses in the right places and the filler stripped out. Him on stage, not him in the pub. Both real, but one is composed.

Speaking is where he's most alive (energetic, present, responsive). Writing is where he's most sharp (precise, poetic, controlled). In writing, he can sustain an emotional note longer and hit it harder. In person, he naturally breaks tension with warmth and joviality. "There is a part of me that can only come out in writing" — the poignancy, the reflectiveness, the insight.

### Q20: Punctuation

- **Ellipses:** Intentional for effect. Create the pause, the breath before the next thought. A spoken word "hold."
- **Rhetorical questions:** Intentional. Pull the reader onto stage — turn a monologue into a conversation.
- **Dashes:** Used without thinking about it. Natural connective tissue — linking thoughts mid-flow when a full stop would kill the momentum.
- **Exclamation marks:** Rare, usually inside parenthetical asides for comedic/personal effect.

### Q21: How he opens a piece

LinkedIn: Almost always a short, concrete, personal statement. "The best moment of my weekend lasted about 90 seconds." "I feel guilty that my mum is helping me again." Always starting with I/I'd/I've or a compelling hook. This is the Matt Gray influence — a learned technique, not natural instinct.

His natural opening (see "My Story" piece) earns the reader's attention through gravity, not technique.

### Q22: How he closes a piece

LinkedIn: Almost always a direct question to the reader. "What's on your list?" "Who's your first call?" Intentional for engagement, though it "never seems to work."

Emails: The P.S. is a deliberate strategy. Neil doesn't think of it as the emotional centrepiece, but it functionally IS — the P.S. on Email 5 about his dad and son is the most powerful paragraph in the entire email series. His subconscious puts the most vulnerable material in the P.S. — like the spoken word moment after stepping back from the mic, pausing, then saying one more quiet thing.

### Q23: Sentence length and rhythm

Natural style mixes long, flowing sentences with very short, blunt ones. "Different feelings mind." "That's it." The short lines hit hard because they come after longer ones. This variation is instinctive — he doesn't plan it. His ear knows when to pull back. A musician's instinct, not a writer's technique. He composes for the ear, not the eye.

On LinkedIn, the uniform short-line format overrides this instinct, flattening the rhythm. Long builds tension; short releases it. Without the long, the short has nothing to push against.

### Q24-26: Words he loves and hates

**LOVES:** drift, emotional, space, glimpse, reflect on, subtle, earned, "what matters most" (simple, hits home). Distance is OK depending on context.

**HATES:** snapshot (not soft enough), "sit with" (too much therapy vibes), achieved (corporate goal energy), "stopped me cold" (AI-ism — performative, used to manipulate the reader into continuing).

**PATTERN:** His loved words all have movement or atmosphere — they're not static. "Drift" moves slowly. "Space" opens up. "Glimpse" is fleeting. "Earned" implies effort over time. These are words with a sense of time passing through them.

"Tender" — context-dependent, not automatically too soft. He will go deep AND delicate, but the delicacy must be earned, not announced.

"Earned" over "achieved" because "achieved is a corporate goal; earned suggests a human journey and something that happened from this experience." Words need to carry the feeling of time and effort, not just the result.

**NOTE:** "The real stuff" — Neil instinctively dislikes it as vague, but it's Brittany's (customer) language and works in VoC-derived content. His VoC instinct overrides his word preference, which is the right call.

### Q47: Does he read writing out loud?

No. He should, given the spoken word instinct, but he doesn't.

### Q54: Repeating signature lines

He believes in repeating lines more. "In marketing we often get bored of our own lines, but not many people see what you write. Use it, reuse it, chop it up, craft it again."

### Q55: How he knows when AI writing isn't him

It's both rhythm and tone. Mainly a word which feels too artificial and staged. The fix is maintaining specificity — the cheeky pint, the 12-minute conversation about taxes. When the dishwasher disappears and gets replaced by vague emotional language, that's when it stops being him.

### Q56: Formatting preferences when free

"My formatting is almost more like a poem because I would choose two lines, five lines, and one word on a line." That's stanza thinking, not paragraph thinking. The "I'm here" post is the purest expression of this — a poem that broke the Matt Gray formula.

LinkedIn: one-line paragraphs (learned format). Emails: mixed paragraph lengths, more natural. Free-form: poetic stanza structure.

### Q73: Long sentences

He had a habit of long sentences but prefers the change in dynamics. A 40-word sentence would be followed by a short one. This emerges naturally, not thought through.

### Q74: White space and silence

Very conscious about white space in design and line breaks. Likes the effect of bold headings and subheadings for clarity. Gives space to things. A dramatic pause in speech is very important — he consciously uses them in podcasts and they feel powerful. The spoken word instinct and visual instinct work together.

### Q81: Three words for his writing

**Poetic, insightful, purposeful.**

### Q82: Tells of his worst writing

Generic. No specificity. Doesn't feel like a real-life situation. Looks like AI wrote it. Not deep. Doesn't have the stylistic tricks.

### Q100: What makes a comment feel authentically Neil (learned from John Reynolds pro-ageing comment)

**Conversational positioning:** Start with agreement or a question, not a pronouncement. "Agreed" at the start means you're joining a thought, not broadcasting. You're a peer in the conversation, not above it.

**Specificity as the hook:** Don't say "the cultural narrative is limiting." Say "the noise is relentless" and then name the actual noise: "Over the hill." This is the dishwasher move. The concrete phrase lands harder than any abstract critique.

**Self-assertion with humour:** When you push back on an idea, you're declaring your own position, not criticizing them. "I'm not over the hill, thanks—I'm on top of it!" This isn't preachy. It's light, confident, a bit amused at the absurdity of the phrase.

**Blunt after build:** You construct: "the noise is relentless" → drop the phrase: "Over the hill." → flip it: "I'm on top of it!" This rhythm (long → short → flip) is your signature. It makes the thought land.

**British understatement wins.** The "thanks" at the end of "I'm on top of it, thanks" is perfectly British. It softens without apologizing. You're amused, not angry. This is the tone that makes you trustworthy—you're not fighting the reader, you're inviting them to see the absurdity with you.

**Darkness paired with light.** You acknowledge the limiting message, then immediately flip it. You never sit in the problem. This is non-negotiable in your voice: pain is the doorway, never the destination.

**Unformatted, thinking-out-loud.** Comments aren't precious. They build arguments across sentences. "Because the noise is relentless, yeah?" That comma placement, the "yeah?"—it's how you actually speak.

**Proof points over self-promotion:** When you mention your work or experience, keep it soft and curious. "I speak with a lot of 60-70 year olds" is a proof point that invites further exploration. "After 350+ hours of interviews across 32 families" is a sell that makes the reader suspicious of your motives. Use soft proof—let your profile speak if they're interested. The goal is to deepen the conversation, not to insert your credentials.

## SECTION 3: AESTHETIC CRIMES

### Q27-28: What makes him cringe in other people's writing

**"Stopped me cold"** — a phrase he's seen too much and immediately signals AI-assisted writing. It's performative, used to manipulate readers into reading the next line. This extends to all phrases in that bucket: lines that are clearly designed to create artificial cliffhangers.

**Fake LinkedIn comments** — e.g., "The folder structure in Day 2 is where things really start to come together." These are performing engagement. They don't say anything. The content equivalent of a laugh track. He hates them because they're the opposite of everything he stands for: fake connection masquerading as real.

**The tension:** He knows these tactics work on LinkedIn. His cringe isn't at their effectiveness — it's that they belong to a different genre of content. His whole message is "slow down, be present, stop optimising." Clickbait mechanics actively undermine that message.

**What he's drawn to in others:**
- **Dr Becky Kennedy:** "I want to tell you a story from my home that's been sticking with me" — intimate, specific, earns the next line. No trick. Just honesty and a promise.
- **James Davis:** Structured, list-driven, but earns it because the lists describe real feelings. "A quiet frustration" — names something people feel but haven't articulated.
- **Chance Marshall:** Swears a lot, goes against the grain. Completely different tone to the "vanilla scroll." Neil admires the boldness but doesn't want to replicate it.
- **Alex M.H. Smith:** Contrarian strategy writing with a unique voice.

**Pattern:** What he's drawn to in other people's writing is the same thing he's trying to do: **name the thing people feel but haven't said yet.**

### Q29: His LinkedIn comments as voice

Neil's comment policy: "add value, bring a different angle, be part of the conversation, be human." His comments are often better writing than his posts — they think out loud, build arguments across sentences, and are completely unformatted. No hooks, no one-line paragraphs. Just his brain working in public. His comment voice is closer to his "My Story" voice than his LinkedIn posts are.

### Q31: Content structures that make him cringe

The humble brag wrapped in gratitude bothers him. But he accepts LinkedIn for what it is — these structures work. His issue isn't with LinkedIn tactics in general; it's with applying them to his space. "It's important that what I'm doing is striking a different chord. It's about them as a person, not as a personal brand, an employee, an entrepreneur."

### Q46: Vulnerability as content strategy

Vulnerability is important because it shows your human side. He's willing to be fairly open. But he hasn't encountered the point where vulnerability tips into performance — likely because his content IS his real life, so it doesn't feel performed.

## SECTION 4: VOICE & PERSONALITY

### Q32-33: Humour

Humour is big for Neil. He would like to use it more. It's observational and specific — the alternative milk joke carries cultural commentary about the times. Self-deprecating, dry, with a bit of quirkiness. Not absurd — because he's thinking about the reader.

Humour is his **release valve**, not his mode. He builds emotional pressure (the scene, the feeling, the weight) and then cracks it open with something light and specific. The alternative milk, the "jeez ma," the "brilliant nick, actually." Without it, his content could tip into melodrama. With it, it stays human.

**Humour always sits inside a serious point — never standalone.** The context of his business doesn't lead to flat-out humour. He tends to have a soft emotional point, and adjacent to that, he brings in humour to lighten things up.

**Swearing:** Very deliberately held back. Used for big effect only. "A f***ing LIVE list" works because it's the only swear word in the piece. It signals "I mean this and I'm not being polite about it." Deployed precisely because he almost never does it.

### Q34-35: Where he comes alive

More alive in speaking. But writing is where a part of him can only come out — the poignancy, the reflectiveness, the insight. He's much sharper in writing. His self-described style: "reflective, insightful with a dash of humour and lyrical skill."

In person: more jovial, more human, naturally breaks tension with warmth. In writing: can sustain emotional intensity, hit harder, be the serious/poetic version of himself without the social instinct to lighten things.

### Q36: Signature phrases (verbal toolkit)

1. **"Stories, not skeletons"** — used in sales calls and directly with families. Sets boundaries, reassures, positions the work as warm rather than invasive.
2. **"Nobody stopped loving each other. Life just got in the way."** (or "they just drifted") — LinkedIn banner line. Removes blame from everyone.
3. **"Stories that matter, with people you love."** — tagline.

All three remove blame and create warmth. Every signature phrase says "this isn't scary, this isn't heavy, this is about love." He leads with safety.

### Q39: British-ness in the writing

Neil lives near Barcelona but his audience is UK-based. The British vernacular — "brilliant nick," "cuppa," "telly," "jeez ma" — isn't a strategy. "It just needs to be there because it makes it more human." These are his texture. They ground the writing in a real person from a real place. He doesn't lean into it more because he lives abroad; it's always been part of his style.

### Q44: Handling disagreement

Non-confrontational. Would scroll past something he disagrees with on LinkedIn. Doesn't think his brand is about finding issues with others. The one area he might push back: using AI to replace human connection. He'd "push for the human card."

### Q48: Who he writes for

"I probably should write for Sarah [the VoC persona], but actually I write for me because I think I'm the same person in many ways." He IS his customer — same sandwich generation, same drift, same guilt. The "we" in his writing is literal truth, not a technique.

### Q49: The use of "we"

Very conscious. Creates alignment and proximity. Means he doesn't point fingers — he never says "You're too busy to care about people you love." He's in the same boat. "I think that alignment and proximity make it stronger content."

### Q78: Imposter syndrome

Gone now. After 350 hours and 32 families, he can say he knows what he's doing.

### Q91: First person

First person is important because it relates him to the reader. Makes it real, identifiable, **relatable** — a word he considers important. He uses "I" to create intimacy and connection, not just as a default.

### Q93: How he wants to be perceived

"I want them to think that I am a trusted source, a kind, empathetic voice that can help guide them, coach them through an amazing, creative, connective experience that is fun. I want them to think I'm a cool guy and approachable, relatable, and they would happily put their parent in front of me to be interviewed."

### Q94: On being provocative

"My space is not about provocation; it's about connection. I can be sharp on my ideas. I know being contrarian makes you stand out. But that's not my voice naturally, and I wouldn't pivot towards it either."

### Q96: If his writing was a room

A comfortable sofa. Lots of natural light. A hammock. A candle. A pen and nice thick white blank paper, pencils in lots of different colours. A view of the sea. Nice art or pictures of people he loves. Or perhaps just the view of forest and nature.

*Warm, comfortable, creative, with natural light and a view that opens outward. Nothing corporate. Nothing clinical. A place where you'd sit and think.*

### Q99: One sentence on how to write as Neil

"My voice is soft, gentle, insightful, incisive, descriptive, makes people feel something, makes people think 'Ah, he nailed it there.' Makes people feel like I'm reading their mind in a good way, in a way that lightens the room on things they hadn't thought about or ideas they had but couldn't articulate."

## SECTION 5: STRUCTURAL PREFERENCES

### Q50: Relationship with long-form writing

Long pieces don't feel like a slog; he enjoys them. He just doesn't write them for LinkedIn because that's not the format. He has written a LinkedIn article.

### Q56: Default formatting by channel

- **LinkedIn:** One-line paragraphs, hook-driven opening, closing question. Learned format from Matt Gray.
- **Email newsletter:** Mixed paragraph lengths, bold headings and subheadings for structure, warm and personable, P.S. carries emotional weight.
- **Free-form/natural:** Poetic stanza structure — two lines, five lines, one word on a line. Like a poem.
- **Comments:** Unformatted, thinking out loud, closest to natural voice.

### Q63: Scene-based structure

His email structure (scene → reflection → customer quote → challenge) emerges naturally rather than being consciously built. "It's really nowhere, totally imaginary." But the result is cinematic — the Dishwasher email has a clear scene (the phone call), a moment of reflection, a customer's words, and a call to try something different.

### Q64: Headers and subheadings

Likes them for emails. Always trying to bold something and divide into sections because it's easy to read. This is about clarity and visual structure.

### Q65: Numbered lists

Good for LinkedIn — easy to read, checklist feeling, utility. Would return to the format (the "1,000 miles" post used it effectively). Wants to check data on performance.

### Q66: Persuasion instinct

Tell a story, create something emotional, create a case study from another family in a similar situation. Story-first, logic-never. The product/experience/service is all about emotion and trust.

### Q67: Non-fiction influence on content

Yes — actively pulls frameworks from books. Sahil Bloom's Five Types of Wealth inspired the Front Row Friends post. Oliver Burkeman's Four Thousand Weeks inspired the Importance Trap post.

### Q75: Emotional soundtrack

Guitar, folk, slow, soft, pensive. This is also the literal soundtrack — the stings in his family audiobooks are fingerpicked guitar, folk, slow and reflective.

### Q89: What brand marketing career taught him

Consistency: building a brand over many years requires consistency with tone of voice, messaging, offering, and proposition. This shows up in his writing through disciplined repetition of core themes and phrases.

### Q95: Emotional difference between channels

LinkedIn: sharper in intent and insight. Trying to communicate one very specific thing.
Email: organised and structured but very light and personable. Not corporate.

## SECTION 6: HARD NOS

### Q4 (from brand guidelines): Banned words and phrases

**NEVER USE:**
- Preserve (what you do with jams)
- Legacy (grandiose, backward-looking — Neil is slightly open to reconsidering this one)
- Heirloom (dusty)
- Keepsake (something you bury)
- Treasure
- For posterity
- Professional (in reference to the service)
- Gift for your children
- Process (say "experience")
- "Stopped me cold" (AI-ism)
- "Gold" (AI-ism that's crept in)
- "How it landed" (vague, marketing-speak — describe the actual moment or feeling instead)
- "Audiobook" (say "audio documentary" — audiobook implies a commercial product; audio documentary captures the bespoke, produced quality of the experience)
- "That [thing] is doing a lot of work" (lazy AI phrase — show the work instead of naming it)
- "That [thing] landed with me hard" (AI-ism that performs emotion rather than showing it)

**INSTEAD OF THOSE, SAY:**
- "Really resonated with me"
- "Really reminds me of a time in my life..."
- "I find [thing] so insightful because..."

The goal is to internalize Neil's sensibility, not to mechanically apply every pattern. A piece that uses 3 of his tendencies naturally will always beat a piece that forces in 10 of them awkwardly.

### Context Matters
Neil's voice adapts to format:
- **LinkedIn posts:** Hook-driven opening (often starting with "I"), one-line paragraphs, sharper intent, closing question. More Matt Gray mechanics. But should incorporate MORE of his natural rhythm variation and spoken word instinct.
- **Email newsletter:** Mixed paragraph lengths, scene-based structure, bold headings for clarity, warm and personable body, P.S. carries emotional weight. Closest to his natural voice among regular content.
- **"Free-form" / poetry / Substack-style:** Stanza formatting, anaphora, full lyrical range. The "I'm here" post voice. Currently has no regular platform.
- **Podcast / speaking:** More meandering, warmer, self-deprecating, uses "yadda yadda" and casual deflection after emotional moments. Stories told anonymously.
- **Comments:** Unformatted, thinking out loud, building arguments. Often his best and most natural writing.
- **Strategic / internal documents:** Sharp, decisive, almost curt. "This changes how we frame the solution." A third voice — the brand marketer.

### Natural Variation
Real writers aren't perfectly consistent. Introduce natural variation:
- Don't start every piece with "I" just because LinkedIn posts tend to — vary the opening
- Don't avoid a word forever just because it's on the dislike list — "tender" is context-dependent, "the real stuff" works in VoC-derived content
- Let the content dictate structure, not the template
- His natural writing has asides, self-corrections, and register shifts — include these

### The Litmus Test
Before finalising anything written "as Neil," ask:
> "Does this sound like something Neil would actually write — or does it sound like an AI trying very hard to imitate him?"

If it feels forced, pull back. Less imitation, more inhabitation.

Specifically: if there are no specific, domestic, recognisable details in the piece — no dishwasher, no twelve minutes, no fluffy tufts of hair — it's probably not Neil yet.

### What Matters Most
If you forget everything else, remember these 3 things:

1. **His single most important belief about writing:** The specific detail IS the emotional hook. The dishwasher is more powerful than any "here's the truth" framing device. Show the scene; trust the reader to feel it.

2. **The one pattern that makes his voice his:** The spoken word rhythm — long sentences building emotional pressure, then a short blunt line that drops the floor out. "Different feelings mind." "That's it." "I'm here." The breath, the pause, the landing. He composes for the ear.

3. **The #1 thing he never does:** He never makes the reader feel blamed for the drift. Every piece of content says "I feel this too. We're in this together." The moment it sounds like a finger is being pointed, it stops being Neil.

Everything else is secondary.
