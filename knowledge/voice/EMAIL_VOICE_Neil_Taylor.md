# EMAIL VOICE: NEIL TAYLOR — ALL EMAIL SUB-TONES

Built 2026-09-23, expanded same day after a scan of the prior week's sent mail. This is the missing piece: VOICE_PROFILE_Neil_Taylor.md covers LinkedIn, the newsletter, and comments in depth, but had no dedicated section for email. Email is not one voice, it's (at least) four, depending on who's on the other end. Read this alongside the main voice profile, not instead of it, every banned word and the core beliefs still apply everywhere.

**Always identify which of the four sub-tones below applies before drafting.** If the recipient relationship is unclear, ask Neil rather than guessing between them, tone-matching the wrong register (e.g. editor-brisk to a grieving fan reply, or fan-warmth to a journalist pitch) is worse than a generic draft.

| # | Sub-tone | Who | Signal |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Incoming reply** | Strangers who wrote in, fans, podcast listeners, curious prospects who've said they're not buying | They emailed first, unprompted, usually emotional or complimentary |
| 2 | **Friend / personal** | People Neil actually knows, old colleagues, mates from TERN, school, past jobs | Existing personal relationship, catching up, not business |
| 3 | **Editor / ops** | Vuk, Abhi, Jodie (editor), anyone doing production work for MMOM | Recurring working relationship, task-and-deliverable focused |
| 4 | **Pitch / outreach** | Journalists, PR contacts, cold or semi-warm intros (Saga, the Independent, Telegraph #journorequest) | Neil wants something from a stranger or a gatekeeper: coverage, a intro, a yes |

## SUB-TONE 1: INCOMING REPLY (fans, strangers, curious prospects)

Built from Neil's own sent edits to three fan/prospect reply drafts (Jamie Daugherty, Bill Bickham, Jodie Schneider — all people who heard him on the John O'Leary podcast).

---

## THE HARD RULE THAT TRIGGERED THIS DOC

**No em dashes. Anywhere. Ever.** Not in a first draft, not in a "just this once" aside. This was already law in root CLAUDE.md and logged twice before (2026-07-14, 2026-07-15) for outreach emails specifically, and it still needs saying a third time. Treat every draft's final step as a mechanical em-dash sweep, no exceptions, regardless of channel.

When Neil himself needs a connective pause mid-sentence in an email, he reaches for:
- A comma: "Thank you for writing, and what a life you've packed into those pages already."
- A full stop, splitting into two sentences.
- A restructure that removes the need for a pause at all.

He does NOT reach for a hyphen-as-dash substitute either, except very occasionally as a genuine list/value separator, e.g. "Stories in people's voices, helping people connect with those they love - that's what I'm here for!" — even there, a comma would have worked. Default to comma or full stop first.

## WHAT NEIL ACTUALLY CHANGED, DRAFT TO SENT

Three examples, same day, same task (thank-you replies to strangers who heard him on a podcast). Comparing Claude's draft to what Neil actually sent reveals the real reply voice:

**Jamie Daugherty reply:**
- Draft: "I imagine John's a good friend" → Sent: "I imagine John is a good friend" (Neil doesn't contract "John's" into a fresh name/subject the way he might contract "I'm" — full form reads more considered here)
- Draft: "Keep doing what you're doing." → Sent: "Congratulations to you, that's an amazing thing." Neil replaced a generic sign-off encouragement with a direct, specific piece of praise for the actual thing they did (wrote a book for their boys). Naming the achievement beats a stock closing line.
- Sign-off: just "Neil", no "All the best" — kept plain when the reply itself already carried the warmth.

**Bill Bickham reply:**
- Draft: "that's a proper library of a life." → Sent: "that's a proper library of a life!" Neil added the exclamation mark. He's more liberal with exclamation marks in warm personal replies than the LinkedIn/newsletter voice profile suggests, they read as genuine enthusiasm here, not hype.
- Draft: "The idea of recording rather than just writing is a good instinct" → Sent: "I really recommend recording rather than just writing" Neil makes it a direct personal recommendation ("I really recommend") rather than a soft observation about their instinct. More direct, more first-person, more like actual advice from one person to another.
- Sign-off: "All the best, Neil" here (contrast with plain "Neil" for Jamie) — the sign-off flexes with the specific warmth of that reply, not fixed template.

**Jodie Schneider reply:**
- Draft: "It's a different thing to hear someone than to read them." → Sent: cut entirely. Replaced with a punchier, more direct value statement: "Stories in people's voices, helping people connect with those they love - that's what I'm here for!"
- Added: "No sales pitch here! :)" — a light, explicit wink acknowledging she'd flagged herself as "not a potential client." Neil meets that directly and with a literal smiley, which he does NOT use in LinkedIn or newsletter copy. Reply-email register allows it.
- The exclamation mark count is notably higher here than his considered/poetic register. Three "!" in a five-sentence email. This is deliberate warmth, not sloppiness, a stranger paid him a compliment and got nothing in return; the register matches "delighted stranger" energy.

## PATTERN: WHAT MAKES A NEIL EMAIL REPLY

1. **Open with direct thanks, immediately.** "Thank you for writing, and..." / "Thank you for this, it genuinely means a lot." No throat-clearing.
2. **Name the specific detail they gave him**, don't paraphrase it into something generic. Nineteen, Czechoslovakia, 63 years, LBD, fourteen years old, all appear because the sender mentioned them. This mirrors the "dishwasher" rule from the core voice profile: specificity over vague warmth.
3. **One line of genuine acknowledgment of loss or hardship where relevant**, plain, not dwelt on. "I'm sorry about your dad." Short, said once, then move forward. Never lingers in the sad beat.
4. **A direct, personal recommendation or reaction, not a hedge.** "I really recommend..." not "you might consider..."
5. **Exclamation marks are allowed and used more freely than in LinkedIn/newsletter copy**, because this register is one real, delighted person replying to another. Two or three per short email is normal here. This is a register-specific exception to the "rare, usually inside parenthetical asides" rule in the core voice profile, which applies to considered/public writing, not quick warm replies.
6. **Sign-off flexes.** Plain "Neil" is fine. "All the best, Neil" when the reply carries more warmth or is going to someone older/more formal (Bill, 90 years old). Match the sign-off weight to the reply's tone, don't default to one fixed line.
7. **No sales pitch, said explicitly when it's relevant.** If the person has flagged themselves as not a prospect, Neil doesn't quietly pivot to selling anyway, he can name it directly ("No sales pitch here!") and mean it.
8. **Short. A five-to-eight sentence email is normal for a fan reply.** Deliverable-defaults rule (one strong draft, no bloat) applies doubly here: these are not newsletter-length pieces.

## WHAT STAYS THE SAME AS THE CORE VOICE PROFILE (sub-tone 1)

- First person, direct address, "I" not corporate "we" for personal replies.
- Specificity over abstraction, always use the concrete detail the other person gave you.
- British phrasing, no American marketing cadence.
- No banned words (preserve, legacy, heirloom, keepsake, process, etc.) even in a two-line reply.
- Never invent, composite, or embellish anything about the other person's story, only reflect back what they actually said.

## SUB-TONE 2: FRIEND / PERSONAL

Built from Neil's own sent emails to people he actually knows: Charlie (TERN alumni), Robert Griffiths ("Griffter"), Katy Evans, and others.

**Examples, verbatim:**
- "Hi maaate, How the bloody hell are you? It's Neil here, new address. Still TERNing it I see. Wow - great dedication to the cause, I salute you. How is life otherwise? You still looking after Fred?"
- "Griffter! Just working my way through the LW replies - great to hear from everyone! Yeah that funeral realisation happens a lot. It's perverse isn't it that we wait until some one is dead to be..."
- "Hey Katy, How's life?! I hope you're well, and having a wonderful and cool summer! All is well here; I've been in Italy with family so half my body is now composed of pizza."
- "Hi mate, Tayls again, my last email probably wasn't timed well. With any luck, you were on a beach somewhere with your phone buried in your beach bag..."

**Pattern:**
1. **Nickname-first greetings.** "Maaate" (deliberately misspelled, stretched out), "Griffter" (surname turned into a nickname), "Tayls" (his own school nickname, self-referential when signing off to old friends who'd know it). This is a register no other sub-tone touches.
2. **Immediate, warm, low-stakes questions.** "How's life, kids, summer plans?" "How the bloody hell are you?" Real interest in specifics of their life (Fred, the kids, the house move), not a scripted "hope you're well."
3. **Self-deprecating asides about his own life**, delivered as a throwaway. "so half my body is now composed of pizza." "my last email probably wasn't timed well... buried in your beach bag."
4. **British informality at full volume.** "bloody hell," "mate," rhetorical exclamation marks stacked ("How's life?!"), contractions everywhere.
5. **Business gets folded in casually, almost apologetically, never leads.** Only after the catch-up does a referral ask or business update appear, and even then it's soft ("I wanted to share a little referral promotion I'm sending out to past families").
6. **Humour surfaces more here than anywhere else** — the observational, dry humour from the core voice profile gets full rein with people who already know him.

## SUB-TONE 3: EDITOR / OPS (Vuk, Abhi, Jodie the editor, production collaborators)

Built from Neil's sent briefing and handoff emails to his three regular editors.

**Examples, verbatim:**
- "Hey Vuk, Phil Taylor S3 - Ch7 & 8 briefing is here. One extra bit of work on this one: could you insert the quickfire questions from the grandkids into the final chapter (Maggie/Ch8)?"
- "Hi Abhi, In parallel to Griffiths feedback, I'm sharing new library of stings. There are 29 in total. Here is the reference doc with links to all stings."
- "Hey Abhi, Did you pick this briefing up? Can you send me V1 by Wednesday end of day CET? I have time Thursday for reviews. If you cannot meet this deadline, please provide your proposal for delivering..."
- "Right, OK, i'm reviewing now. I have a meeting with Geoff/son to hand over everything Wednesday 5pm so I want to have final approved in good time for that. Feedback will be with you before 6pm CET."
- Sign-offs and acks are often a single word or short line: "Approved! Thanks Abhi" / "great thanks" / "Excellent received. Thanks" / "Great, looking forward to it"

**Pattern:**
1. **Brisk, task-first, no warm-up.** No "hope you're well." Straight into the deliverable, the rate, the deadline. This is the leanest of the four sub-tones by far.
2. **Numbers are exact and stated plainly.** Rates in £, running times to the minute, deadlines with day and timezone (CET). Ops precision, not poetic vagueness.
3. **Short imperative or direct-question sentences.** "Could you insert..." "Can you send me V1 by..." "Please confirm receipt."
4. **Accountability language when something's slipping, still calm, not tense.** "Did you pick this briefing up?" "If you cannot meet this deadline, please provide your proposal for delivering" — direct, unemotional, solution-seeking, never passive-aggressive.
5. **Acknowledgment replies are often one line, sometimes one word.** "great thanks", "Approved!" This is functional correspondence between collaborators who trust each other, not persuasion.
6. **Still has warmth, but it's efficiency-as-warmth**, not relational warmth. Being clear, fast, and fair to their time IS the kindness in this register.
7. **Never a sales register with this group.** They work for him. No performance, no persuasion, no inspiration. Pure clarity.

## SUB-TONE 4: PITCH / OUTREACH (journalists, PR, cold or semi-warm gatekeepers)

Built from Neil's sent pitches to Saga (Kirsty Tyler), the Independent (Vicki Harper), and the Telegraph (#journorequest to Jack Rear). Cross-reference: lessons.md 2026-07-14 and 2026-09-05 entries already cover length and banned-phrase discipline for this register specifically.

**Examples, verbatim:**
- "Hi Kirsty, Catherine Carr gave me your details. She's just published her book on Siblings (those who get along, those who don't, and why). When we got talking it turned out I do for a living..."
- "Hi Jack, I saw your call for a parent who sent their child to boarding school and later regretted it. I run Me & My Old Man, where we record conversations between parents and their adult children..."
- "Hi Vicki, Hope you had a good summer. As promised, I'm touching base to discuss the Christmas run-up timing for The Family Documentary, for you or one of your writers. Given lead times (~10-12 weeks)..."
- "Hi Vicki, I know things get buried, so here's a quick reminder: I run Me & My Old Man, we make audio documentaries of parents' life stories. We interview adult children and their parents..."
- "Christmas run up sounds good. I'll touch base again early September. Have a great summer!"

**Pattern:**
1. **Always name the connective tissue first, if there is one.** "Catherine Carr gave me your details." A warm intro beats a cold one, always lead with who sent you or what specific hook triggered the email (their callout, their article, their book).
2. **One clear, concrete offer, stated early, not built up to.** Never a menu of "angles." A single link, a single ask, a single sentence describing what MMOM actually is.
3. **Short. Genuinely short.** These pitches run 3-6 sentences. Busy gatekeeper, one ask, no padding, this is a hard rule already logged twice (2026-07-14, 2026-07-15).
4. **Repeats himself plainly on follow-up, doesn't get cute.** "I know things get buried, so here's a quick reminder" is Neil re-stating the pitch almost verbatim rather than inventing a new angle. Persistence here looks like patience, not escalation.
5. **Accepts a soft no or a delay gracefully, in one line.** "Christmas run up sounds good. I'll touch base again early September. Have a great summer!" No pushing, no over-explaining, just confirms the new timeline and signs off warm.
6. **No banned words even here** — "preserve," "legacy," "audiobook" etc. are still off-limits in a pitch, say "audio documentaries," "The Family Documentary," "record conversations between parents and their adult children."
7. **No em dashes, doubly enforced here** — this register produced the two prior logged em-dash failures (2026-07-14 Independent pitch, 2026-07-15 Lei reply). Sweep every pitch draft last, before it goes out.
