# Lead Magnet Formats — The Playbook

Every Me & My Old Man lead magnet falls into one of four formats. Pick the one that matches the reader's mental readiness and the complexity of the idea. Don't mix formats in one asset.

---

## 1. PDF Guide

**When to use:** The topic needs a bit of depth — 2-5 minutes of reading. The reader wants to think, not just do. Best for TOFU-to-MOFU bridge assets.

**Length:** 1,200-2,500 words. 6-12 pages when designed.

**Structure:**

1. Cover page — title + promise line.
2. Intro (150-250 words) — Neil's voice. Surface-detail hook. Name the problem. Promise the payoff.
3. Body — 5-10 short sections. Each section = one idea. Each section has:
   - A title (short, declarative, no cleverness for cleverness's sake)
   - A one-line reframe
   - 80-150 words of depth
   - A try-this or reflection prompt
4. Closing reflection (100-150 words).
5. Soft CTA.
6. About MMOM (75-100 words).

**Do:** Use subheadings generously. Stanza-style paragraph breaks. One big idea per page when possible.

**Don't:** Write a blog post with a cover on it. Don't lecture. Don't list frameworks.

**Example titles:**
- "The Drift Audit: 7 signs your family is drifting — and what to do about each one"
- "The Closed Book Dad: how to get a quiet parent to open up, one question at a time"
- "What I've Learned From 350 Hours of Family Interviews"

## 2. Prompt Sheet

**When to use:** Pure utility. Reader wants something they can pick up tonight and use tomorrow. Highest conversion for TOFU — it's the most obviously useful.

**Length:** 10-15 questions. 2-4 pages designed.

**Structure:**

1. Cover page — title + a one-line setup for why these questions work.
2. Short intro (75-125 words) — Neil names the problem (e.g. "I ask 'how was your day', they say 'fine', we both go back to our phones") and promises that these 12 questions break the pattern.
3. The questions — grouped into 3-4 themes. Example themes:
   - The warm-up (easy, surprising, invites a smile)
   - The middle depth (draws out story)
   - The door to the room you've never walked into (the vulnerable ones)
   - The close (one they'll reflect on after you've gone)
4. Each question has:
   - The question itself (bold)
   - A one-line note: *"Why this works: …"* or *"What to listen for: …"*
5. Closing note (50-80 words) — Neil's gentle permission-giver. *"You don't need to ask all twelve. One well-asked question is worth more than a dozen half-asked ones."*
6. Soft CTA.

**Do:** Ground every question in the voice the reader will actually use. Make them sayable out loud. Include 2-3 truly surprising ones.

**Don't:** Write interview-show questions. Don't include anything that sounds therapeutic. Don't use "tell me about a time when…"

**Example titles:**
- "10 questions that will unlock your dad in one conversation"
- "The 12 Questions: a free prompt sheet for your next phone call home"
- "7 questions you haven't asked your mum yet (and should)"

## 3. Checklist / Framework

**When to use:** The topic has steps. The reader wants to know if they've covered something. Best for pre-call / pre-visit / pre-trip scenarios.

**Length:** 7-12 items. 1-2 pages designed (often printable).

**Structure:**

1. Cover / top of page — title + promise.
2. Two-sentence intro (nothing more).
3. The checklist — each item has:
   - A checkbox (visual)
   - A short declarative statement (5-10 words)
   - One sentence of depth / instruction beneath it
4. Bottom of sheet — a single reflection question and a soft CTA.

**Do:** Make it genuinely printable. Think fridge-magnet energy. Think: something a reader pins above their desk.

**Don't:** Make it 25 items. Don't write "step 1 of the 7-step framework for…"

**Example titles:**
- "The Legacy Conversation Checklist: 9 things to cover before it's too late"
- "Before You Visit Mum: a one-page checklist for the drive home"
- "The Story Capture Kit: what to bring to a conversation with your dad"

## 4. Email Mini-Series (3-5 days)

**When to use:** The topic is emotional and benefits from spacing. The reader needs to sit with each idea before the next one arrives. Best for mid-funnel nurture. Also useful when Neil wants to test an idea before it becomes a guide.

**Length:** 3-5 emails, 300-500 words each.

**Structure (5-email example):**

| Day | Job | Tone |
|-----|-----|------|
| 1 | Welcome + the honest reason you're here | Warm, personal, Neil's founding moment |
| 2 | Name the drift (the specific, domestic detail that names the problem) | Vivid, uncomfortable, true |
| 3 | The reframe — the new way to see it | Gentle, insight-led, earned |
| 4 | The small action — what to try this week | Specific, sayable, doable |
| 5 | The wider lens — what this is really about | Standalone line, quiet close |

**Each email follows the newsletter skill's movement structure:** HOOK → RECOGNITION → REFRAME → BRIDGE → TEASE/CTA. Read `About Me/.claude/skills/newsletter/SKILL.md` (or the copy Neil keeps in his system) for full detail.

**Do:** Send one email per day. Keep subjects in sentence case, under 50 characters. Always end with a P.S.

**Don't:** Make it an 8-email series. Neil's read rates fall off a cliff after email 5. Don't save the best email for last — front-load the strongest emotional beat in email 2.

**Example titles:**
- "The Drift: a 5-day invitation to reconnect"
- "Before It's Too Late: 5 emails on the conversations worth having"
- "Rooms You've Never Walked Into: 5 stories, 5 questions, 5 days"

---

## Which Format When — Quick Decision Tree

- Reader needs **utility tonight** → Prompt Sheet
- Reader needs a **think** → PDF Guide
- Reader has a **specific moment coming up** (a visit, a call, a trip) → Checklist
- Reader needs **permission and time** → Email Mini-Series

If still unsure, default to Prompt Sheet. It converts best for cold audiences and is the fastest to ship.
