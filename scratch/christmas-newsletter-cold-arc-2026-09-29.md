## 1. Fri 30 Oct: the launch

Subject: What did you want at eight?
Preview: A football, a Father Christmas, and a jet with no cockpit.

Hey,

I asked a man in his seventies what he wanted for Christmas when he was a boy.

A football, he said.

He remembered being at the market, being asked what he'd like, and his mum steering him away so his dad could go and "send it off to Father Christmas."

He got the football.

Then, without me asking, another Christmas. An Airfix kit of a Hawker Hunter jet. Seventy years on, he could still name the plane.

He'd built most of it. He left out the little bits inside the cockpit, because he couldn't be bothered. "I think my mum and dad were quite upset about that."

I've become a bit obsessed with this question. "Tell me about your childhood" gets you a paragraph. "What did you want when you were eight, and did you get it?" gets you a toy, a market, a dad, and a mum who knew what she was doing.

Try it this Christmas. Ask, then stop talking.

I've put the best Christmas questions into a short list you can keep in your pocket: [CHRISTMAS QUESTIONS LINK]

Neil

P.S. My dad's last Christmas was 2020. I never asked him what he wanted at eight. You've got a Christmas coming up to ask yours.

## 2. Tue 24 Nov: 31 days

Subject: 31 days. Home at two on Christmas Day
Preview: Fourteen-hour days on the Christmas post.

Hey,

31 days to Christmas.

A man I recorded told me about the Christmas job his dad got him.

His dad was a postman. Every year, while he was at college, his dad got him onto the post for the run-up. Two and a half weeks. Fourteen-hour days, seven days a week, for the money.

In those days there was a delivery on Christmas morning, so he did that too.

He got home about two o'clock on Christmas Day, absolutely shattered. Something to eat, then bed.

His family knew he was a postman's son. I doubt any of them had thought about what Christmas was like for him, or for his dad, from the other side of the front door.

That's the thing with Christmas stories. We know ours from the kitchen. We rarely know what it looked like when they were the ones making it happen.

Here's one to try before the day itself:

"What was Christmas like for you when you were the one doing the work?"

Then wait. The first answer is usually the practical one.

The questions I've been collecting are here, if you want them on the table: [CHRISTMAS QUESTIONS LINK]

Neil

P.S. You don't need an afternoon. One question is enough to find out something you didn't know.

## 3. Tue 1 Dec: 24 days

Subject: 24 days. Her first midnight service
Preview: She was sixteen. She asked to go on her own.

Hey,

24 days to Christmas.

A dad read me a poem in one of our sessions. He'd written it on Christmas morning, 1991.

The night before, his daughter, sixteen, had asked if she could go out on Christmas Eve with her friends. On her own. First time.

He and his wife said yes.

"As we slowly filled her Christmas pillowcase," he wrote, "we each silently wondered if this would be the last Christmas Eve we would need to do this."

Then they watched the midnight service on the telly, until a light knock on the door. Cold cheeks. "Happy Christmas, mum, happy Christmas, dad."

He finished with this: "Our gift to her that Christmas Eve was our trust and our love."

It took about a minute to read out. It's on the recording now, in his voice.

Most of what parents feel on nights like that never gets written down. They don't say it, because in the moment there's a pillowcase to fill and a kettle to put on.

Neil

P.S. The line I keep coming back to is "She's our child, but not our child. She belongs to the world." A dad wrote that on a Christmas morning, and now his family can hear him say it.

## 4. Tue 8 Dec: 17 days

Subject: 17 days. "I wasn't pre-empting a Christmas list"
Preview: A son, a dad, and two dream machines.

Hey,

17 days to Christmas.

At a wrap-up session, the whole family together, a son asked his dad a question I'd never have thought of.

His dad had always talked about two things: a dream road car and a dream racing motorbike. The son asked which one he'd actually have.

His dad's answer: neither, really. He doesn't like motorcycles in museums. They're made to be ridden. A road bike, not a race bike.

And the son, quick as anything, added: "I wasn't pre-empting a Christmas list."

It might be the most Christmassy thing anyone said all year.

Christmas gives us a permission we don't have the rest of the year. It's the one time we're allowed to ask what someone wants. So ask the other version too: what did you always want, and never got?

Neil

## 5. Fri 11 Dec: 14 days

Subject: 14 days. Yorkshire pudding for pudding
Preview: What he remembered about Christmas. And what he didn't.

Hey,

14 days to Christmas.

I asked a man what he remembered about Christmas as a boy.

Parties at his auntie's house, a small house, packed, full of distant relatives he could never quite place. A tape recorder, new that year, and an uncle setting it up to tape the family.

He didn't remember Christmas trees at all.

What he did remember was the table set against the wall at home. Him, his mum and his dad, eating Christmas pudding. And on an ordinary night, Yorkshire pudding, but as the dessert, with sugar sprinkled in the middle.

I found myself wondering where that tape went. Most of us have a version of it: the party where somebody said "we should record this," and nobody did, or somebody did and it went missing.

That's the whole reason I do this.

## 6. Tue 15 Dec: 10 days

Subject: 10 days. What to get the man who has everything
Preview: He listed his favourite wines. I said that's Christmas sorted.

Hey,

10 days to Christmas.

Near the end of a session, I asked a dad in his seventies about his favourite things.

A white from Italy he'd been drinking lately. A Barbera for a red. A little tot of Armagnac now and again.

"Well," I said, "at least the kids know what to get you now."

But the better bit was that he tied the Barbera to a walking holiday in Italy. The wine was the excuse. The holiday was the story.

Every Christmas we buy for the parent who says "don't fuss, I've got everything." A jumper, a bottle, another book.

The thing they haven't got is somebody sitting with them for an afternoon, asking where they had it first.

If that's your parent, it's worth a 20-minute chat about what a few afternoons with them could turn into: https://calendly.com/meandmyoldman/20min-family-story-discovery-session

Neil

P.S. Ask what they'd pick, then ask where they first had it. You get a present and a story for the price of one.

## 7. Fri 18 Dec: 7 days

Subject: 7 days. Christmas 2020
Preview: We thought we'd do it next year.

7 days to Christmas.

My dad's last Christmas was 2020. We didn't know.

We thought we'd sit down and record his stories the year after.

He died in March 2021. My son was born six days later.

I can't hear his voice now. Not properly. It went quicker than I thought it would.

I don't tell you that to make you feel anything. I tell you because Christmas is the one week the whole family ends up in one room, with time on its hands and a parent at the table who's a bit more relaxed than usual. It's the easiest week of the year to ask.

So pick one person. Pick one question.

## Old series review (8 emails, Dec 2024)

Verdict: keep almost nothing. The strategy of a countdown into Christmas survives, and so does the idea of a Christmas question list. The copy does not.

Kept, in new form:
- "What did YOU want for Christmas when you were 8? Did you get it?" is now email 1, answered by a real story.
- "12 Questions of Christmas" as the magnet (two questions reused in email 8).
- Christmas 2020 with your dad, now email 7, with hope after it and no "you'll be gone" arithmetic.

Binned:
- The maths email, the "Christmas 2028: Dad's gone" timeline and "This could be your last Christmas": all fear without hope, and a countdown to catastrophe.
- "Stop reading and book the call": a command close.
- The Jon story (72, rural Wales, two daughters, the wrap-up where "everyone cried"). Nothing in the brain matches it and it reads composited. If it's real, tell me which family. If not, it stays out.
- "Laura tells me..." and "the family story concierge" and "You do nothing. I do everything."
- Banned or dated words: audiobook, legacy, "12 weeks of your family getting closer" as a pitch, "limited January spots," "6 families for January."
- Twice-weekly hard-ask cadence from 1 Dec. The subscriber who said "American-style marketing" was right.
