Lapsed 1: Why are you messaging me?

Subject: "Why are you messaging me?"
Preview: That was her brother's first reply. Three months later, a different story.

Hey,

When one sister first suggested doing this with their parents, she sent a message to her brother.

His reply, in full: "Why are you messaging me?"

Not hostile. Just a man who didn't normally hear from his sister unless it was about Christmas or a birthday, suddenly getting a message about their mum and dad's life story. It didn't compute.

Three months later, he was the most engaged person in the whole family. He described the experience as "tying the knot again."

I think about that first reply a lot. Because it's the bit nobody warns you about.

Most people I speak to on a call aren't unsure about the idea. They're unsure about the sentence. The one you have to say out loud to a brother, a sister, a parent who'll probably tell you their life isn't that interesting. There's a version of you that's already signed up in your head, and a family that has no idea any of this is happening.

That gap is normal. It's also exactly where most people go quiet.

So I wrote down the sentence. Plus the six things people usually say back, and what you can say to each. "Is this because you think I'm dying?" is in there. So is "Why don't you just ask me yourself?"

Week 1 (The Drift): Drawing blood from a stone

Subject: "I only hear from him when he wants something"
Preview: Two siblings. Same mum. Same house.

Hey,

I spoke with a daughter last month. Her brother lives in London too, opposite end, and they barely speak. About real stuff, anyway.

"I only hear from him when he wants something," she said.

Not angry. More like a fact she'd made peace with a long time ago.

They did the recordings anyway, separately, then longer sessions with Mum and Dad. And afterwards, she said the best part wasn't her parents' stories.

It was hearing her brother.

"The most I'd heard from him in years was his wedding speech," she told me. "Drawing blood from a stone, normally."

Two siblings. Same mum. Same childhood house. Completely different memories of it, and completely different ways of not talking about it since.

Nobody stopped loving anybody. Life got in the way, and then it kept getting in the way.

We don't drift from our siblings on purpose. It just happens, even in close families.

Week 2 (Pain Points): The Sunday run

Subject: Every Sunday since she was seven
Preview: She thought it was about the running.

Hey,

A daughter told me she's been running with her dad every Sunday since she was seven.

She still does. Every week, whatever the weather.

I asked what she thought she got out of it. She said it probably started as him trying to keep her fit. Then she stopped, and said actually, no. She didn't think it was ever about the running.

So I asked what she thought he got from it.

She didn't have an answer straight away. Which was its own kind of answer.

That's the thing about the one-on-one things our parents did with us. The Saturday trip to the tip. The same café after swimming. The car rides where the radio did most of the talking. We rarely ask why they did them. We just did them, grew up, and stopped asking.

Here's one thing to try on your next call. Pick the thing the two of you used to do, and ask:

"Why did you used to take me to [the thing]?"

Then stop talking. Count to ten if you have to. The first answer is usually the practical one. The second one is the real one.

Week 3 (The Transformation): The lizards

Subject: A shoebox of lizards, mid-flight
Preview: "Don't you dare say anything."

Hey,

I was there for a story about lizards. That's not what I expected to write down.

He was in his eighties. Grew up in Cyprus, always out catching snakes and lizards as a kid. He'd tuck a black one under his shirt and take it into school. Swap it for a steak sandwich. Use it to terrify the girls in his class.

Then he told me about the plane.

His family flew to the UK on holiday, and he smuggled a shoebox of live lizards on board, tucked by his feet. Mid-flight, he kicked it. The lizards got out.

His mother looked at him and said one thing: "Don't you dare say anything."

Poor lizards.

I've sat with people telling me about the biggest days of their lives. Marriages. Wars. The day they lost someone. None of it made me laugh the way the lizards did. And his family had never heard it. Forty years, and it had never come up.

That's the bit people don't expect. It isn't only the big stuff. It's the shoebox on the plane, and the mother who never said a word.

Week 4 (The Now): Two pages about a house

Subject: Two pages about the house name
Preview: Then twenty years, gone, in half a sentence.

Hey,

A man I spoke to recently told me his dad spent the last years of his life trying to write his own biography.

He got two pages in. Both of them were about why he'd named the house what he'd named it.

Then nothing, until a line that skipped from the year his son was born to the year that son left for university. Twenty years, gone, in half a sentence.

I hear a version of this all the time. The unfinished memoir in the drawer. The voice memo that sat on someone's phone and never came off it. My own dad, who I tried to get down on paper, one slow email at a time, until dementia closed the door on it. Twelve pages, and none of them sounded like him.

I think we get this wrong. We treat a life story like a writing project, something the person does alone, in a voice that sounds like a Wikipedia entry and nothing like them.

But nobody talks the way they write. The bloke who'll keep you in the pub for an hour with one story goes stiff the second there's a blank page in front of him.

A life story isn't written. It's told. Out loud, to someone who's actually interested, who asks "and then what happened?" at the right moment and lets the silences breathe.

If your mum or dad has been "meaning to write it all down for the grandkids," hit reply and tell me how far they've got. I'd love to know what's in the drawer.

Neil

P.S. If they've got as far as a title and nothing else, that's normal. Two pages is quite good, honestly.
